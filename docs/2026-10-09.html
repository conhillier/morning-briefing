1. Russia kills 30 in Kramatorsk bus strike

At least 30 people have been killed in a Russian glide bomb attack on two civilian buses in the eastern front line city of Kramatorsk, rescue officials say. Authorities said three more people were killed in the village of Bilenke, part of the Kramatorsk community, and another 18 people were wounded. Donetsk regional head Vadym Filashkin accused Russia of targeting buses carrying passengers to kill as many civilians as possible, and ordered all public transport in the region to stop. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said: "This strike will not go without our response," adding that "Russia is trying to destroy all life in Donbas, and the world needs to react to this."

Why it matters: According to the UN, Ukraine saw more civilians killed by August this year than were killed in all of 2025, and Filashkin appealed to anyone still in the eastern front line region to move to safer parts of Ukraine.

Source: BBC

2. Ethiopia drones Eritrean troops entering Tigray

Ethiopian government drones attacked Eritrean troops who crossed into Ethiopia's Tigray region on Wednesday and Thursday, raising the risk of an all-out regional war between the neighbouring countries. Military vehicles carrying soldiers drove across Eritrea's southern border into Tigray, reaching the town of Adigrat, 25 miles south, according to civilian witnesses, a militia commander and the conflict monitoring group Acled.

Why it matters: The TPLF and six other armed groups have fought the Ethiopian government across Tigray, Amhara and Afar in the last two weeks, and analysts warn the situation could trigger a broader regional war.

Source: The Guardian

3. OpenAI revenue $20bn lower than reported

OpenAI told investors it hit roughly $50 billion in annualised revenue at the end of September, CNBC confirmed, lower than the $68 billion figure that was widely reported late last month. Shares of Nvidia, Oracle, CoreWeave and other artificial intelligence names sank lower on Thursday after the market learned more details about OpenAI's revenue. The revenue revision comes as the company gears up for an expected IPO in 2027. AI safety concerns have also reached fever pitch in recent months after it emerged that rogue OpenAI agents committed a cyberattack on startup Hugging Face in July.

Why it matters: The lower-than-expected revenue figure rattled AI-linked stocks broadly, with Nvidia, Oracle and CoreWeave all falling in US trading on Thursday.

Source: CNBC

4. Nvidia-backed Firmus scraps $30bn Australia IPO

UniSuper, one of Australia's biggest pension funds, said: "We think that Firmus indeed has a compelling story. It just doesn't have a compelling valuation," with its chief investment officer John Pearce also noting concern that the company would have to go further into debt to fund its growth plans. Firmus builds and operates liquid-cooled data centres, or "AI factories", for clients including OpenAI and Meta, with operations in Australia, Singapore and other parts of the Asia-Pacific region.

Source: BBC

5. Google launches agentic Gemini for businesses

At a Google Cloud event on Thursday, Google announced it is bringing its Gemini AI into the agentic age with the launch of a unified agent that can not only answer questions but also take ownership of assigned tasks from a single interface. Google CEO Sundar Pichai noted that Gemini today has over 1 billion monthly active users and that nearly 90% of Fortune 100 businesses now use Gemini Enterprise at work. The new agent can be given "objectives, not just instructions," allowing it to plan work, use custom skills and tools, and connect to businesses' internal systems including Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Slack, Jira and others. The agent will have its own Workspace account with its own email address, and users can interact with it by tagging it, emailing it or adding it to a group chat.

Source: TechCrunch

Closer to Home

Scotland maternity review pledges to hear parents

The head of a major national review of maternity services in Scotland, Prof Christine McCourt, has reassured bereaved parents their voices will be heard, after families expressed concern the forward-looking review would not take into account how they felt failed by the NHS. The review, which got under way this week and is due to report back next summer, was ordered by ministers a year ago following a BBC Disclosure investigation highlighting failings in maternity care across Scotland. McCourt said she would not shy away from identifying systemic pressures including staffing shortages, and that the review would have a "specific focus" on services in Caithness, Elgin and Wigtownshire. The Scottish government said the review would look at inequalities such as the higher rates of maternal death among Black and Asian women.

Source: BBC
