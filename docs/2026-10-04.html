1. Russia hits second Kyiv bridge in days

A Russian strike hit the Pivnichnyi (Northern) Bridge in Kyiv on Saturday morning, injuring two people and damaging the road surface and overhead trolleybus wires, Mayor Vitaliy Klitschko said. The attack follows multiple drone strikes on the Pivdennyi (Southern) Bridge earlier in the week, with Klitschko warning that "the enemy is tearing Kyiv apart". The two bridges across the Dnipro River link the western and eastern parts of the capital and had not been hit before Thursday. Russian strikes have severed road, rail and trolleybus links across the wide river, bringing traffic jams to the city's roads for a second day.

Why it matters: Experts say repeated attacks will force the bridges' closure and risk weakening their structure, making them unusable, while the fear is this new Russian tactic could effectively cut the city of roughly three million residents in two.

Source: BBC

2. G7 releases 100 million barrels to ease fuel crisis

The G7 has announced 100 million barrels of oil and diesel will be released to ease supply concerns, with the move beginning immediately and lasting four months. The package includes a "substantial release" of diesel within 20 days, with discussions around additional diesel releases as necessary. French President Emmanuel Macron chaired the meeting, saying the co-ordinated action would "bring down the prices of petroleum products, particularly diesel", with releases co-ordinated through the International Energy Agency. The price of global benchmark Brent crude briefly dropped below $100 a barrel but rose back to around $102 by Friday evening, after it had been trading around $73 before the US and Israel invaded Iran.

Why it matters: Avoiding a ban on US diesel exports offers significant relief to countries reliant on imports, including the UK, where prices at the pump topped £2 a litre for the first time on Friday, with over half of UK diesel imported and 31% of those imports coming from the US.

Source: BBC

3. Trump names AI czar to lead new task force

Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton has been chosen as the Trump administration's AI czar, leading a new White House task force called the "Super Intelligence Force" - the term President Trump prefers over AI - according to The Wall Street Journal. Clayton told the Journal the group will have 120 days to research and report on AI's risks and opportunities and offer recommendations on the federal government's responsibilities regarding the technology. Clayton is a former chair of the Securities and Exchange Commission and US attorney for the Southern District of New York, and was confirmed by the Senate in July as DNI, giving him authority over 18 US intelligence agencies. The announcement comes days after AI industry leaders met with Trump and House Speaker Mike Johnson at the White House, where developers signed onto an agreement of voluntary safety standards Trump described as "morally binding".

Why it matters: Despite the new task force, Trump has so far opposed regulation of AI, and in a September post suggested AI fears were a hoax.

Source: CNBC

4. Australia probes Flydubai co-pilot's links to country

Australian authorities are investigating the links between the country and the Flydubai co-pilot who attempted to take control of a plane travelling to Israel on 30 September, after the UAE prosecutor general said he had attempted to carry out a "terrorist act". The Australian Federal Police confirmed its investigation into "the man's links to Australia", with the probe also involving state police and the country's security agency. Sources familiar with the case told Australian media the co-pilot was issued a student visa in 2014 and studied in Australia between 2015 and 2017 until he left, with the visa cancelled in 2019 as he was no longer enrolled in a course. Counter-terrorism officials will look at whether the man was radicalised in Australia, according to the Australian Broadcasting Corporation.

Source: BBC

5. US completes military withdrawal from Iraq

The US military has completed its withdrawal from Iraq, 23 years after its invasion toppled Saddam Hussein, with the Iraqi government declaring a national holiday and holding celebrations. At the height of the US military presence there were 170,000 troops, but that number had fallen to about 1,500 at the time of withdrawal. The US had previously withdrawn in 2011 when Iraq was relatively stable, but was invited back three years later to help fight ISIS. Iran-backed militias have painted the departure as a victory, while the Iraqi prime minister has promised President Trump he will disarm those militias - though a deadline for that has moved into next year.

Why it matters: There are significant worries in the Kurdistan region, which has been hit hard by Iranian attacks, with a feeling of uncertainty and betrayal at the US departure.

Source: NPR

Closer to Home

Energy suppliers demand urgent UK bill action

Industry body Energy UK has called on the government to take immediate action on energy bills, warning that inaction would lead to "a longer-term, more costly and deeper crisis". Households in England, Scotland and Wales on variable tariffs were hit with a 4% price increase at the start of October, taking a typical annual bill to £1,723, while consultancy Cornwall Insight forecasts the same typical bill could rise to £1,999 in January - a further 16% rise flagged by forecasters earlier in the week. Energy UK said savings from VAT cuts on electricity bills and cancelled levies had been wiped out by high wholesale prices driven by conflict in the Middle East and disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said the government was "looking at any measure that can give people breathing space, that can take the pressure off".

Source: BBC
