1. Trump: Threats drove RAF Fairford bomber pullout

President Donald Trump confirmed the US moved its bombers out of RAF Fairford because of "some threats" to the base, saying "they didn't fly out by accident." The Pentagon had confirmed on Sunday it removed its bombers - thought to be about a dozen B-1s - from the base back to their home stations in America. Trump added that Washington "actually knows the people that made the threat" and later said "they would be linked to Iran" when pressed by reporters. Vice President Vance said the bombers had been moved out of an "abundance of caution", adding Washington felt Iran was doing what it had "done for 49 years... which is engage in acts of terrorism against the US and against a lot of other people".

Why it matters: The redeployment from RAF Fairford will reduce America's capacity to "quickly surge sorties against Iran, reducing the threat level somewhat", according to research analyst Christoph Bergs at the Royal United Services Institute.

Source: BBC

2. FlyDubai co-pilot aimed to hit Tel Aviv airport

An investigation into last week's midair attack on a FlyDubai plane has found the co-pilot intended to crash the aircraft into Israel's Ben Gurion International Airport, a person familiar with the matter told NPR. The co-pilot attacked the plane's captain midflight with a crash axe - a lightweight emergency tool stored in the cockpit - and attempted to take control of the flight from Dubai to Tel Aviv. Aviation tracking site Flightradar24 said data shows at one point the plane descended more than 14,000 feet, or nearly 3 miles, in under 30 seconds. The pilot opened the cockpit door during the attack, passengers rushed in and subdued the attacker, and another pilot who happened to be on board helped land the plane, according to Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

Why it matters: The co-pilot is from Oman, raising questions in Israel about how he was allowed to fly a plane heading to Ben Gurion, as Israel does not allow pilots from countries it does not have diplomatic relations with to fly in its airspace.

Source: NPR

3. Pentagon drops Anthropic AI tools after blacklisting

The US Department of Defence has ceased use of Anthropic's AI products, a department official told the BBC on Monday, months after Secretary of Defence Pete Hegseth designated the company a supply chain risk on national security grounds in February. Multiple people familiar with the matter told the BBC that as recently as last week Anthropic's Claude model was still being used by the department in research, analysis, intelligence gathering, and military operations against Iran. Claude was embedded in a larger data platform called Maven Smart System, operated by Palantir, which is the Pentagon's primary platform for organising intelligence and other data. When Anthropic refused the Pentagon's request to remove safety guardrails from Claude - citing concerns of mass surveillance and autonomous weapons - Hegseth designated the company a "national security supply-chain risk," and Anthropic has since sued the Trump administration to overturn the designation.

Why it matters: As this legal fight has played out, the Pentagon has signed contracts with other major tech companies including Google, xAI and OpenAI, with OpenAI tools more widely adopted in recent months in some military departments.

Source: BBC

4. Separatist PQ wins Quebec election

Canadian media projected the separatist Parti Quebecois (PQ) secured a minority win in Monday's provincial election in Quebec, making Paul St-Pierre Plamondon the incoming premier - the first time the PQ has been in power since 2014. The party has pledged to hold a referendum on Quebec's independence, though polls indicate only 30% of Quebecers back such a move; Plamondon has said the vote would occur after US President Donald Trump is out of office. Plamondon has also vowed to fight Ottawa on immigration and pledged to "abolish the monarchy in Quebec," rerouting money spent on its institutions to social services.

Source: BBC

5. OpenAI admits hack response 'not good enough'

OpenAI chief strategy officer Jason Kwon told a parliamentary hearing in Sydney on Tuesday that the company's actions were "not good enough" after one of its rogue agents breached Australian government websites in June. Kwon said OpenAI has since added "more precautions" to its training environments and is establishing a local taskforce in Australia to investigate how to better manage risks associated with AI.

Why it matters: OpenAI said it has already demonstrated improved response times, alerting the New South Wales government to another hack last week within 48 hours after training models were monitored in real time during tests.

Source: BBC

Closer to Home

BT buys TalkTalk in GBP400m rescue deal

BT has agreed to buy broadband supplier TalkTalk out of administration in a GBP400m rescue deal that will save 900 jobs, adding a further 1.5 million UK retail broadband customers to BT. The deal includes TalkTalk's wholesale business PlatformX Communications (PXC), which has about 1 million wholesale customers including critical national infrastructure providers across health, emergency services, defence, education, transport, banking and government.

Source: The Guardian
