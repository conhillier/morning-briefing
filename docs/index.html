1. Trump declares 'energy ceasefire', Russia silent

US President Donald Trump announced that Russia and Ukraine had reached an "energy ceasefire", saying it was "effective immediately" but providing no details. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky expressed initial surprise before saying Kyiv would support it if Moscow did, writing that his officials "look forward to clear details of America's dialogue with Russia". However, Zelensky later said Russia hit a power plant in Kyiv with a drone and struck energy facilities in the Kharkiv region after Trump's announcement. UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham said Britain "strongly supports President Trump's call today for a sustained energy ceasefire to start now", while France, Canada and the EU also expressed support.

Why it matters: The ceasefire claim came against a backdrop of months of exchanges of strikes on energy infrastructure on both sides, with attacks having disrupted energy supplies for both Ukraine and Russia.

Source: BBC

2. Senators slam Trump Russian diesel deal

A group of seven senators - four Republicans and three Democrats - said Trump's deal to import Russian diesel violated US laws, urging him to "reverse course". Trump announced on Friday that Putin had agreed to release 300,000 tonnes of diesel immediately, followed by 500,000 tonnes in November and one million more after that, with a further three million tonnes to follow. The average diesel price in the US is currently $6.28 a gallon, down from the record high of $6.53 recorded at the end of September. The statement was signed by Republicans Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Thom Tillis and John Curtis, as well as Democrats Jeanne Shaheen, Richard Blumenthal and Ron Wyden.

Why it matters: The rising fuel prices have become a key issue for Americans ahead of next month's midterm elections, which will determine if Trump's Republican Party maintains control of Congress.

Source: BBC

3. Second Houthi strike hits Riyadh airport

One person has been killed and 11 injured in a second strike on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, with a Saudi military spokesman saying a projectile hit the airport causing a fire in a passenger hall. Saudi Arabia's civil aviation authority confirmed the victim was a Pakistani resident. It follows a strike the previous day on the same airport that killed 13 people and injured 308 others, described as the deadliest single attack on a Gulf Arab country since the war between Israel, the US and Iran began in February. Houthi military spokesman Brig Gen Yahya Saree vowed to continue "escalating operations deep inside Saudi Arabia", warning that all Saudi airports were potential targets apart from those in Mecca and Medina.

Why it matters: US President Donald Trump said the US would "make a decision" and "move very quickly" on whether to strike the Houthis, while UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting suggested the UK could "potentially" provide more support for Saudi Arabia.

Source: BBC

4. Poland detains man in Tusk assassination plot

Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said a man accused of plotting an assassination attack against him and Defence Minister Wladyslaw Kosiniak-Kamysz has been detained. The suspect was arrested in the Lower Silesia region and was identified as a Polish national, according to Jacek Dobrzynski, a spokesman for the Special Services Co-ordinator. The plan was uncovered when an email with information about the potential attack was forwarded to police by the State Protection Service, which is responsible for ministers' security. Tusk thanked the police for "swift and decisive action" in arresting the suspect.

Source: BBC

5. IMF warns AI is a 'slow-moving tsunami' for jobs

IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva warned on Monday that AI was hitting the global labor market like a "slow-moving tsunami", with young graduates first in line to be affected, estimating that about 40% of jobs worldwide would eventually be "augmented, downgraded or eliminated". Exposure is about 60% in advanced economies and 23% in low-income countries. Singapore President Tharman Shanmugaratnam, co-chair of the World Bank's Jobs Council, called for governments to make "crisis-era" support measures a permanent tool for getting young people into work, noting that "the first rung in employment, your first job upon graduation, is now seeing less demand". World Bank President Ajay Banga estimated 1.2 billion young people will turn 18 over the next 15 years, with the private sector needing to absorb most of them.

Source: CNBC

Closer to Home

Scottish A&E doctor 'terrified' ahead of winter

Dr Fiona Hunter, vice chair of the Royal College of Emergency Medicine Scotland, said A&E departments were providing "unsafe" levels of care, with some patients spending 72 hours on trolleys in hospital corridors. She warned that emergency staff across the country were being asked to look after "essentially a ward's worth of extra patients" with some departments working at 500% capacity but without 500% extra staff, leading to high rates of sickness and burnout. She also said that month-on-month, almost 2,000 patients were experiencing delayed discharge across Scotland. Health Secretary Angela Constance acknowledged winter would be "difficult" but reiterated that corridor care was unacceptable and said she wanted to move towards a system where preparing for winter was a "year-round" activity.

Source: BBC
