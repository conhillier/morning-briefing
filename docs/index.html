1. AI stock rout erases Nasdaq 2026 gains

US stocks suffered a brutal week of selling, with the Nasdaq Composite shedding 5.28% on Friday to close at 22,617.73, turning negative 2.7% year to date. The S&P 500 dropped 3.7% to 6,910.39 and the Dow fell 949.73 points, or 1.93%, to 48,147.33, with both the S&P 500 and Dow entering correction territory, moving 10% below their closing records. Anthropic lost 8.5% on the day, while chipmakers AMD and Nvidia slid more than 13% and around 14% respectively, with Nvidia also recording its longest weekly losing streak since the 2008 financial crisis. For the week, the Dow fell 8%, its worst weekly performance since March 2020, the Nasdaq finished down about 10%, and the S&P 500 decreased 8.6%, its worst week since April 2025.

Why it matters: "This is not just a valuation-driven correction. It's becoming broader," said Massimo Santicchia, head of U.S. equities at Procyon, with the potential for more interest rate hikes from the Federal Reserve also spooking traders.

Source: CNBC

2. COP31 deadlocked over fossil fuel language

Negotiations at the COP31 UN climate summit in Antalya, Turkey ran through the night and remained in deadlock as countries failed to agree on a new global climate deal. A group of countries led by France and Panama pushed back against a draft agreement that included no reference to reducing demand for fossil fuels, with fossil fuel producers led by Saudi Arabia having pushed for its removal. "It is not acceptable to have a COP or a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels and we simply will not put up with it," said Juan Carlos Navarro, Panama's minister for the environment. For the first time at a COP, the agreement - known officially as the Antalya Accords - acknowledges that the goal of keeping global temperature rise below 1.5C has been missed.

Why it matters: Earlier versions of the text included an agreement to ramp up money for adaptation to $120bn by 2035, but that has since been removed, leaving developing nations without the financial commitments they sought.

Source: BBC

3. Trump threatens ban on overseas data centres

US President Donald Trump said he will ban American companies from building AI data centres overseas, threatening a "tremendous tax" for tech firms who do so. "We're not going to allow our companies to build data centres outside of our country," Trump told reporters, saying "China will take all of them" if the US does not act. The announcement follows a move earlier in the week that would require firms to obtain a licence to buy advanced chips, so US officials can monitor whether they are being used to serve Chinese companies or smuggled into China. Trump called out specifically Google's €13bn investment in AI infrastructure in Finland, saying his administration was "not allowing that to happen".

Why it matters: The policy would affect major tech firms including Meta and Google, which have already entered agreements to build data centres overseas, though how such a policy would be enforced remains unclear.

Source: BBC

4. US cyber chief fired after tech outages

White House National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross has been removed from his post after little over a year in the job, with President Trump saying it was because he was not "good enough". Cairncross was fired after losing the confidence of White House chief of staff Stephen Miller, according to CBS. His firing follows a string of tech outages which hit banking, air traffic control and other sectors, though US officials said the outages were not caused by cyberattacks. Secretary of State Marco Rubio posted on X to say Department of Defense chief information officer Kirsten Davies was the new national cyber director.

Why it matters: The outages sparked scrutiny from members of Congress who demanded a clearer explanation of what went wrong, and the rapid succession of incidents has left some people worried about the safety of technology relied on in all aspects of daily life.

Source: BBC

5. Anthropic employee claims Riemann hypothesis proof

An Anthropic employee and mathematician, Levent Alpoge, claims to have used the company's Claude chatbot to prove the famed Riemann hypothesis, one of the most significant unsolved problems in mathematics. Alpoge said he used a non-public version of Claude over the course of about six weeks to create the detailed proof, describing it as "one dawn across science of something far greater". The complete textual proof, written in Lean - a computer language used for confirming maths proofs - is over 250,000 pages long. However, prominent mathematician Terrance Tao said on Friday that verification of the proof is still "some way off".

Why it matters: The Riemann hypothesis is a nearly 170-year-old maths problem dealing with prime numbers and is widely considered to be the single most famous problem in maths; if the proof is confirmed correct, it would be among the most significant in history.

Source: BBC

Closer to Home

Two men die in Loch Leven glider crash

Two men have died after a glider crashed into Loch Leven, with police, ambulance, fire service and the coastguard called to the scene near Kinross at about 11:00. The Scottish Gliding Centre, based at Portmoak Airfield near the loch, said a two-seater Perkoz aircraft had "crashed whilst on a local training flight" but it was not clear what caused the crash. Police Scotland said the deaths were not being treated as suspicious and inquiries were ongoing. The Air Accidents Investigation Branch (AAIB) has launched an investigation and deployed a multi-disciplinary team including inspectors with expertise in aircraft operations, engineering and recorded data.

Source: BBC
